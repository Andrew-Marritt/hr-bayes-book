The recurring image

A mockup of the visual device for the meetup talk: one engine, then the same posterior shaded to answer four different business questions, then multiplied by an elicited cost to give impact in money. Colours are the book’s own (Chapter 4): pale blue = prior, red = likelihood, navy = posterior. All numbers here are illustrative — they need a render of the book to be real.

The engine — shown once, then a small motif in the corner of every slide

This appears in full at about minute 5, and then shrinks to a strip in the corner of each of the five examples. The claim it makes visually: every model in the talk is the same three steps. Only the labels change.

Prior

What we believed before

“Around 30%, but I’d not be shocked by 20 or 45.”Benchmarks, last year, or your VP’s informed opinion.
+
Likelihood

What this data says on its own

“17 promotions in 42 people.”The evidence, taken at face value.
→
Posterior

What we believe now

“About 36%, and here is how sure we are.”One object. Every question below is asked of this.
Why this earns its place. It converts “Bayesian” from a philosophical position into a picture with a verb. The audience doesn’t need to follow the arithmetic — they need to see that there is one object at the end, and that everything afterwards is a question asked of it.

The payload — four questions, one distribution

Build these on one slide, revealed in sequence, at about minute 6. The whole sequence takes ninety seconds. It is the single highest-value ninety seconds in the talk, because every one of the five examples afterwards is a callback to it.

Question 1

How much, and how sure?

“About $578k a head, and we’re 95% sure it’s between $525k and $631k.”Shade the middle 95%. This is the credible interval — and unlike a confidence interval, that sentence means what the listener thinks it means.
Question 2

What’s the chance we clear the bar?

“There’s a 21% chance they average above $600k.”Shade above the number the business cares about. No refitting — you can do this in the meeting, while they’re asking.
Question 3

Is the trained group genuinely higher?

“A 95% chance the trained group is genuinely ahead, by about $58k.”Two posteriors. Subtract them draw by draw and you get a posterior for the difference — which is the next panel.
Question 4

Is the gap big enough to act on?

“An 82% chance the uplift beats the $25k we said we’d need to see.”Shade beyond the threshold you set before looking. A significance test can only ever ask about exactly zero.

The contrast — put these side by side

This is the moment the “why Bayesian” question answers itself, without any argument about philosophy. Chapter 20’s line does the work: “There is no way to ask it ‘how likely is the gap bigger than three percentage points’, because it only ever tests against exactly zero.”

The alternative

What a significance test hands you

p = 0.03
Significant. Reject the null.
There is nothing here to shade. You cannot ask it “what’s the chance we clear $600k?” or “is the gap bigger than $25k?” It only ever tests against exactly zero — and every new question means a new test.
The point

One object, asked four different ways

You build the shape once. Every business question after that is a different way of shading it.“If you can phrase a question about that vector, you can answer it — and you can do it after the fact without refitting anything.” — Chapter 5

The finale — the same verb, one last time

At about minute 21, back on the coaching decision. The new element is the elicited cost distribution on the left — which is just a prior, pointed at a price instead of an effect. Multiply, subtract the fee, shade above zero.

Elicited

What one extra dollar of sales is worth

“Somewhere around 22c in the dollar, and I’d be astonished above 60c.”Nobody has measured this. So ask finance three questions and fit a distribution to the answers — the same object as a prior.
×
Impact

What the programme is worth, in money

“A 61% chance it pays for itself. Median return $102k, and a real chance of losing $200k.”Multiply the two distributions draw by draw, subtract the cost, and shade above zero. Same verb as every panel above.
Why the finale lands. By this point the audience has watched you shade a distribution five times. The multiplication is the only genuinely new idea in the slide, and it arrives as the obvious next move rather than as new machinery. That is the whole argument for making the device recurring.
